# The weekly routine

Fifteen minutes, in a fixed slot, right before a meeting that already happens. A new standalone calendar event gets lost.

The board needs three things: the slot, one owner who scans it, and the time box.

## The three questions, in order

**What changed since last week?** Read the change note. Glance at any card it mentions. Nothing else.

**Does any change affect our next three videos?** The decision question. Most weeks the answer is no, and a fast no is a fine outcome.

**Is the competitor list still right?** Channels stall, pivot, or stop mattering. Every month or so, ask whether a slot should go to someone new. Edit `competitors.csv` and the next refresh picks it up.

## Sharing it

Different roles read different sections. Whoever plans content wants topic direction and outliers. Whoever owns spend wants growth rates. A founder wants the change note and the position paragraph.

Keep one board rather than building three. If someone needs the same slice every week, add a line to the standing instruction so every refresh produces it.

## The risks, in short

- **The numbers are estimates.** Third-party providers measure from outside. Trust directions and ratios. Never quote exact figures externally as fact.
- **One week means little.** Act on movement that holds for two or three refreshes.
- **The giant's playbook is a trap.** Weight the channels closest to your size and stage.
- **Lists go stale with no warning.** That is what the monthly list check is for.

## Where the automation stops

The board tells you what moved. It cannot tell you why it worked. Before rebuilding a format around what a competitor is doing, watch a dozen of their videos and form a judgment. Pacing, teaching style, and warmth appear in no metric.

The rule binds harder when money is involved. Repositioning, pricing changes, and paid campaigns can be informed by the board, but a person checks the underlying videos and pages before anything is spent.

Everything this board touches is public: published videos, public channel statistics, data a provider measures from outside. Keep it there. The five-question compliance check from Chapter 1 applies.

## Two caveats specific to platform data

- **Platform counting changes break comparability.** YouTube changed how it counts views in August 2026, which inflated long-form counts afterward by roughly 1.7 to 1.9 times. A video published before that date and one published after are not directly comparable. When a platform changes how it measures, note the date in your positioning notes so a later refresh does not read a counting change as growth.
- **Recent videos have not finished accumulating.** A video four days old sitting below channel average may end up well above it. Treat anything under two weeks old as incomplete, and do not let it pull an outlier list.
