# Interview scorecard and decision framework

Brightpath Analytics is the fictional company used throughout Chapter 3. Add this file to the Hiring project context.

**The 1 to 4 scale.** Each level has its own description, and the per-question rubric in the guide sharpens these for the specific question.

- **4, strong:** clear, specific evidence the candidate has done this, with a concrete example and a result or a decision attached.
- **3, adequate:** solid evidence and a sound decision, with less detail on how they verified it or one trade-off left unaddressed.
- **2, light:** correct understanding of the topic without evidence of having done it. Familiarity, or experience from a supporting seat.
- **1, weak:** no real evidence, vocabulary without substance, or an answer that raises a concern.

**Writing a rubric level.** Two tests. Does the level require something this role would actually give a person access to? Could two interviewers apply it to the same answer and reach the same number? A level that fails the first rejects good candidates. A level that fails the second puts private judgment back into the process.

**Decision rule.** A candidate is a hire when they pass every knockout, average 3 or higher across the four scorecard areas, and score no lower than 2 on any single priority. A score of 1 on a priority is a no-hire unless the hiring manager records a reason to override. A strong candidate with one thin priority is a conditional hire: proceed only if the interview produces a plan to close the gap.

The total at the bottom of the printed scoring form is a quick read, not the decision. The decision comes from the four area averages and the floor of 2 on each priority, because a high total can hide a 1.

**Panel scoring.** Each interviewer owns one question group, so most questions are scored once, by one person. The debrief assembles the three sections into a single scorecard and reads it by area, not by argument.

**Calibration.** Because the split means nobody scores the same answer twice, pick one question per group to be scored by all three interviewers. Compare those three numbers at the debrief. Where two of them differ by more than one point, the interviewers reread their evidence lines and agree on the score the evidence supports, then fix the rubric wording that allowed the spread. That is how the rubric stays a shared standard rather than three private ones.
